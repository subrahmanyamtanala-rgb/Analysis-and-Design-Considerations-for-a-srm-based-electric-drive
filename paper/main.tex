\begin{abstract}
This paper presents an analytical, finite-element (FE) and simulation-based
design methodology for a switched reluctance motor (SRM) drive for a 250-W,
25-km/h pedelec supplied from a 36-V battery. The vehicle mission is translated
into motor torque--speed requirements, and the phase turns are chosen at a
mission-derived \emph{corner speed} rather than at the assist cut-off speed.
The resulting relation $P_c=k_cV_{dc}I_{pk}$, a sizing form of the classical
converter volt-ampere argument, is tested quantitatively. Across nine designs
spanning a 3.9-fold range of active mass, the required peak current shows no
mass dependence ($R^2=0.004$) and $k_c=0.60\pm0.03$. Across 24-, 36- and 48-V
redesigns, the current scales as $V^{-1.00}$. A 12/8 machine is sized
analytically and its characteristics are computed with a nonlinear 2-D FE
model. This numerical verification shows errors of the analytical model of up
to 23\% in flux linkage and 17\% in mean torque, so FE tables are used for all
drive-level predictions. Three-phase FE solves confirm that mutual coupling
changes mean torque by at most 1.5\%. The predicted performance of the
$\varnothing$135-mm, 3.4-kg (active) machine with an 8:1 gear is as follows. It
meets the 5.93-N$\cdot$m launch torque at 26.5~A with an 11.6\% margin at any
battery voltage between 30 and 42~V. It reaches 80.2\% motor and 77.9\%
battery-to-shaft efficiency at the rated point and consumes 2.5--10.9~Wh/km on
flat, hilly and aggressive missions. Cubic torque-sharing control cuts torque
ripple from about 197\% to 10--33\% below 500~r/min, at a cost of 7--12
efficiency points. An equal-envelope analytic surface-PM benchmark is about
12 points more efficient at the rated point and uses less energy on hilly and
aggressive missions. The SRM uses less energy on the flat light-assist
mission, needs no magnets and has no drag when coasting. No prototype
measurements are reported, and all performance figures are model predictions.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{E}{lectric} bicycles are the most widely sold electric vehicles
worldwide. Their drives are almost exclusively brushless permanent-magnet (PM)
hub or mid-drive motors. PM machines offer high torque density and efficiency,
but they rely on rare-earth magnets. Those magnets carry supply-chain and price
risk, limit operating temperature and add drag torque when unpowered (cogging
and eddy-current losses). This last point matters for a pedelec, which by
regulation must be pedaled and is ridden unassisted above 25~km/h~\cite{en15194}.

The switched reluctance motor (SRM) addresses these concerns. Its rotor is a
stack of laminations, its stator carries concentrated coils, and its phases are
electrically and magnetically independent to first order. This gives inherent
fault tolerance and zero torque when the windings are not
excited~\cite{miller1993,krishnan2001}. SRMs have been studied extensively for
electric and hybrid vehicles~\cite{rahman2000,xue2010,bostanci2017,jiang2017,lan2021,bilgin2019}.
Their known drawbacks are torque ripple, acoustic noise and lower efficiency at
small power ratings~\cite{husain2002,vujicic2012,fang2021}. Classical design
procedures~\cite{krishnan1988,radun1995,vijayakumar2008}, pole-number
studies~\cite{bilgin2012} and geometry-optimization methods~\cite{abdalmagid2022}
exist, but most of them target kilowatt-class machines with DC-link voltages of
several hundred volts. Light-vehicle work has addressed torque
control~\cite{hamouda2020}. For e-bikes specifically, Terzi\'c and Mihi\'c
designed, built and tested a 6/10 SRM for a mid-drive replacing a commercial PM
machine~\cite{terzic2022}. The \mbox{250-W}, 36-V hub-drive regime combines a
severe volt-second limit, a launch torque about four times the rated torque, a
pancake hub envelope and a mission dominated by part-load cruising. To the
author's knowledge, the combination of mission-derived corner-speed turn
selection, FE-based current sizing, and converter, thermal and control
evaluation has not been reported systematically for a 250-W, 36-V geared SRM
pedelec drive.

This paper contributes the following:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A mission-driven corner-speed rule for choosing the turns of a
low-voltage SRM. Its consequence $P_c=k_cV_{dc}I_{pk}$ is positioned relative
to established volt-second and converter volt-ampere arguments and is tested
quantitatively against mass (nine designs) and design voltage (24/36/48~V).
\item A mission-to-converter design flow that combines analytical sizing with
nonlinear 2-D FE numerical verification (including three-phase coupled solves),
FE-based current sizing and commutation-angle optimization.
\item FE-based performance, loss, converter, DC-link and transient thermal
predictions, with sensitivity to battery voltage and state of charge, iron-loss
model, PWM frequency, gear efficiency, heat-transfer coefficient, ambient
temperature, air gap and pole arcs, evaluated on three standardized missions.
\item A quantified comparison of current-reference and torque-sharing-function
(TSF) control, and an equal-envelope, equal-boundary comparison with an analytic
surface-PM benchmark.
\end{enumerate}
The scope is deliberately limited to analysis, FE simulation and system
simulation. No hardware results are reported, and all efficiency, range and
temperature figures are predictions under stated assumptions
(Section~\ref{sec:limits}). All code, FE scripts and data are openly
available~\cite{repo}.

\subsection{Road-Load and Gear Models}
The tractive force at the tire contact patch is
\begin{equation}
F = C_{rr} m g\cos\alpha + m g \sin\alpha + \tfrac12 \rho C_dA\, v^2 + k_m m\, \dot v,
\label{eq:roadload}
\end{equation}
where $m=100$~kg (80-kg rider plus 20-kg bicycle), $C_{rr}=0.006$,
$C_dA=0.5$~m$^2$ (upright rider), $k_m=1.05$ accounts for rotating inertia,
and $\alpha=\arctan(\text{grade})$. With wheel radius $r_w=0.33$~m, the motor
speed is $\omega_m=Gv/r_w$. The gear is modeled with a load-dependent mesh
efficiency $\eta_g$ and an optional load-independent drag loss $P_{nl}$
proportional to speed, i.e., a constant drag torque $T_{nl}=P_{nl}/\omega_{b}$:
\begin{equation}
T_m = \frac{F r_w}{G\,\eta_g} + T_{nl}.
\label{eq:motortorque}
\end{equation}
The baseline uses $\eta_g=0.95$ and $P_{nl}=0$. Section~\ref{sec:route}
reports the sensitivity to both parameters.

\subsection{Corner-Speed Design Rule}
\label{sec:corner}
In single-pulse operation, the peak flux linkage a phase can reach is limited by
the volt-seconds applied during the dwell angle $\theta_d$:
\begin{equation}
\psi_{pk} = \frac{V_{dc}\,\theta_d}{\omega}.
\label{eq:voltsec}
\end{equation}
The number of turns per phase follows from $\psi_{pk}=T_{ph}B_{sp}A_{p}$, where
$A_p$ is the stator-pole face area and $B_{sp}$ the design pole flux density.
The average torque is $T=(mN_r/2\pi)W_c$, where $W_c$ is the energy converted
per stroke, i.e., the area of the $\psi$--$i$ loop. With a loop-utilization
factor $k_c=W_c/(\psi_{pk}I_{pk})$ and $\theta_d$ equal to the stroke angle
$\varepsilon=2\pi/(mN_r)$, the power at the speed $\omega_c$ where the turns are
chosen is
\begin{equation}
P_c = T_{pk}\,\omega_c = \frac{mN_r}{2\pi}\,k_c\,\psi_{pk} I_{pk}\,\omega_c = k_c\,V_{dc}\,I_{pk}.
\label{eq:invariant}
\end{equation}
The turns are chosen at the corner speed $n_c=P_{pk}/T_{pk}=805$~r/min
(12.5~km/h) with $P_{pk}=500$~W, because the launch torque is only needed at
walking speed. Above $n_c$ the drive operates at approximately constant power.
Under the assumptions of \eqref{eq:invariant}, which are a one-stroke dwell, a
fixed $V_{dc}$, the chosen corner power and a similar $k_c$, choosing the turns
for full launch torque at the 25-km/h base speed would instead require
$P_c=T_{pk}\omega_b\approx1$~kW. This is twice the corner power, and therefore
approximately twice the peak current. Section~\ref{sec:trade} tests the rule
quantitatively and finds $k_c=0.60\pm0.03$ across nine design variants.

\subsection{Relationship to Existing Volt-Second and Energy-Based Methods}
\label{sec:novelty}
Equation~\eqref{eq:invariant} is built from established ingredients. The
volt-second limit \eqref{eq:voltsec} is the basis of every SRM single-pulse
analysis~\cite{miller1993,krishnan2001}. The energy-conversion-loop picture
underlies Miller's energy ratio and the classical argument that the converter
volt-ampere rating per unit output power is fixed by how well the
$\psi$--$i$ loop is utilized~\cite{miller1993}. In that sense,
\eqref{eq:invariant} is the converter volt-ampere argument rewritten in
sizing form, and we do not claim it as a new law. The established design
procedures~\cite{krishnan1988,radun1995,vijayakumar2008,bilgin2012} differ in
how they are used. They size the machine from the output equation
\eqref{eq:output}, which contains the magnetic and electric loadings $B$ and
$A$ but not the supply voltage, and then choose the turns at the rated or
base speed.

What this paper adds is how the relation is used, which matters at low voltage:
\begin{itemize}
\item The turns are chosen at a corner speed derived from the mission
($P_{pk}/T_{pk}$), not at the speed limit.
\item Rearranged as $I_{pk}=P_c/(k_cV_{dc})$, the relation shows that the converter
current is fixed before the machine is sized, and that enlarging the machine
reduces copper loss but not current. Section~\ref{sec:trade} confirms this
quantitatively: the required current shows no mass dependence over a
3.9-fold range of active mass and scales as $V_{dc}^{-1.00}$ when the design
voltage is changed.
\item $k_c$ is reported for this machine class, which allows a first converter
rating directly from vehicle data.
\end{itemize}

\section{Topology Selection and Machine Design}

\subsection{Pole Configuration}
Table~\ref{tab:poles} compares candidate configurations at the maximum speed.
The 12/8 machine is selected under the stated envelope, frequency, converter
and performance constraints; it is not the result of a global topology
optimization. It has the same number of strokes per revolution as the
four-phase 8/6 but needs only six switches. Its two pole pairs per phase
balance the radial forces and shorten the flux paths, which suits the short,
large-diameter hub envelope. Its phase frequency (257~Hz) keeps iron losses
moderate with 0.35-mm laminations, whereas 16/12 and 24/16
machines~\cite{bilgin2012,jiang2017} double this frequency at no benefit for a
250-W machine. The 6/4 machine has a 30$^\circ$ stroke, which causes large
torque dips and makes starting on a grade more difficult.

\subsection{Main Dimensions}
In torque form, the SRM output equation~\cite{krishnan2001,krishnan1988} reads
\begin{equation}
T = \frac{\pi}{4}\,k_e\,k_d\,k_2\,B\,A\,D^2L,
\label{eq:output}
\end{equation}
where $D$ is the bore diameter and $L$ the stack length. The coefficients are
$k_e=0.85$, $k_d=0.9$, $k_2=0.7$, $B=1.7$~T and $A=45$~kA/m (short-term peak
electric loading). For $T_{pk}=5.93$~N$\cdot$m this gives $D^2L=184$~cm$^3$. An
aspect ratio $L/D=0.5$ and a split ratio $D/D_o=0.53$ give $D=71.7$~mm,
$L=35.9$~mm and $D_o=135.3$~mm. We use \eqref{eq:output} only as a starting
point; the current required to meet $T_{pk}$ is determined later with the FE
characteristic (Section~\ref{sec:flow}).

\subsection{Pole Arcs}
The stator and rotor pole arcs must satisfy the feasibility conditions of
Lawrenson \emph{et al.}~\cite{lawrenson1980}:
\begin{equation}
\min(\beta_s,\beta_r)\ge \varepsilon, \quad \beta_s\le\beta_r, \quad \beta_s+\beta_r<\frac{2\pi}{N_r}.
\label{eq:lawrenson}
\end{equation}
We select $\beta_s=15^\circ$ and $\beta_r=17^\circ$, close to the lower-left
vertex of the feasible region in Fig.~\ref{fig:geometry}(b). This choice
maximizes slot area and the inductance ratio. Table~\ref{tab:arcs} shows the
FE-computed sensitivity at constant turns. Widening both arcs by 2$^\circ$
(17/19) raises the mean motoring torque at 30~A by 9.7\% but raises $L_u$ by
14\% and takes slot area from the winding. Widening only $\beta_r$ to 22$^\circ$
lowers torque. A full co-optimization of pole arcs and winding is left to
future work~\cite{abdalmagid2022}. The baseline is kept because it maximizes
copper area for this low-voltage, copper-loss-dominated design.

\subsection{Poles, Yokes, Turns and Winding}
The stator yoke is $y_s=0.75\,w_s$, where $w_s=9.4$~mm is the stator-pole width.
The pole flux divides in two in the yoke, so $0.5\,w_s$ is the minimum. The extra
margin limits yoke saturation and stiffens the stator against the ovalizing
vibration mode~\cite{miller1993}. The rotor-pole height is limited to $30g$
($g=0.3$~mm). With \eqref{eq:voltsec} at $n_c$, the minimum battery voltage of
30~V and a one-stroke dwell, $\psi_{pk}=93.2$~mWb. This gives $T_{ph}=164$
turns, i.e., 41 turns on each of the four poles of a phase. Bobbin-wound
concentrated coils with a net fill factor of 0.45 use 1.66-mm wire, and
$R_{ph}=0.198~\Omega$ at 100~$^\circ$C. Table~\ref{tab:design} summarizes the
design. All masses are \emph{active electromagnetic} masses (laminations and
copper), excluding housing, shaft, bearings and gear.

\subsection{Analytical Inductance Estimates}
The aligned unsaturated inductance follows from two air gaps in series per pole
pair, with all $p=N_s/m$ poles of a phase in series:
\begin{equation}
L_a = k_{fe}\,\frac{\mu_0 (w_s+2g) L\, T_{ph}^2}{p\,g},
\label{eq:La}
\end{equation}
where $k_{fe}=0.9$. The unaligned inductance combines the path into the rotor
slot ($g+h_r$) with two side paths ($l_s$) and an end-winding factor
$k_{ew}=1.35$:
\begin{equation}
\frac{L_u}{L_a} = \frac{k_{ew}}{k_{fe}}\left(\frac{g}{g+h_r}+\frac{g}{l_s}\right).
\label{eq:Lu}
\end{equation}
These estimates are compared with nonlinear FE in Section~\ref{sec:fea}.

\section{Electromagnetic Modeling and FE-Based Numerical Verification}

\subsection{Analytical Saturable Flux-Linkage Model}
For fast design iteration, the aligned magnetization curve is represented by an
exponential knee model in the spirit of~\cite{torrey1990,ilic1987}:
\begin{equation}
\psi_a(i) = L_{sat}\,i + \psi_k\left(1-e^{-(L_a-L_{sat})i/\psi_k}\right),
\label{eq:psia}
\end{equation}
with $\psi_k=B_kA_pT_{ph}$ and $B_k=1.55$~T. It is blended with the linear
unaligned characteristic by a $C^1$ position function $w(\theta)\in[0,1]$:
\begin{equation}
\psi(i,\theta)=L_u i + w(\theta)\left[\psi_a(i)-L_u i\right],
\label{eq:psi}
\end{equation}
which gives closed-form co-energy and torque,
$T=\tfrac{dw}{d\theta}\big[W'_a(i)-\tfrac12L_ui^2\big]$.

\subsection{Nonlinear 2-D FE Model}
\label{sec:fea}
The full 12/8 cross-section is modeled with the magnetic vector potential
$A_z$ on first-order triangles:
\begin{equation}
\nabla\cdot\big(\nu(|\mathbf B|^2)\nabla A_z\big)=-J_z,\qquad A_z=0\ \text{on}\ r=D_o/2,
\label{eq:fe}
\end{equation}
solved by Newton iteration (relative residual $<10^{-6}$, typically 3--12
iterations) with a monotone PCHIP fit of the M270-35A DC magnetization curve.
The geometry is meshed for every rotor position, with an element size of $g/3$
in the air gap growing to 1.2--1.5~mm in iron and air (about $2.9\times10^4$
nodes). Flux linkage is computed from the coil-side averages of $A_z$, and an
analytical end-winding term $L_{ew}=p\,\mu_0N_c^2l_{ew}\lambda_{ew}$ with
$\lambda_{ew}=0.5$ is added. Torque is computed by Arkkio's method over the whole
air-gap annulus~\cite{arkkio1987}. The table covers 16 rotor positions
(0--22.5$^\circ$, mirrored to a full pitch) and 15 currents (1--42~A).

Table~\ref{tab:mesh} shows mesh convergence. Halving the gap element size from
$g/3$ to $g/5$ changes flux linkage by less than 0.1\% and torque by 0.03\%. As
an independent consistency check, the torque obtained by differentiating the
FE co-energy agrees with the Arkkio torque to within 0.5\% in mean value.

\subsection{Analytical Model Versus FE}
Fig.~\ref{fig:feaval} and Table~\ref{tab:feaval} compare the analytical model
with FE at 8, 15, 23 and 31~A. The analytical model overestimates $L_a$ by
5.7\% and underestimates $L_u$ by 9.7\%, so the true inductance ratio is 5.6
rather than 6.6. Its smooth-step position function also underestimates the flux
linkage in partial overlap by up to 23\%, because it neglects the fringing that
lets flux build up before geometric overlap. Mean motoring torque is therefore
underestimated by 2--17\%, while peak static torque is within $\pm$10\%. The
knee model is accurate near 23~A but over-saturates at 8~A and under-saturates
at 31~A. These errors exceed what the original analytical design assumed.

the FE characteristic}, imported as dense $\psi(i,\theta)$ tables with torque
computed from the co-energy of the same table, so the drive model stays
energy-consistent. The analytical model is retained only for the trade study of
Section~\ref{sec:trade}, where it is used for relative comparisons.
Fig.~\ref{fig:field} shows the flux distribution at mid-overlap, with the
pole tips saturated locally.

\subsection{Mutual Coupling}
\label{sec:mutual}
Mutual coupling was evaluated by FE with phases A and B excited simultaneously
(15/15, 30/30 and 30/10~A) at seven positions. In the commutation overlap
($\theta\le8.5^\circ$, when the outgoing phase is near alignment), the second
phase changes $\psi_A$ by at most 7.2\%. The torque differs from the
superposition of single-phase torques by at most 0.36~N$\cdot$m (6.9\%). The
largest deviations, up to 0.65~N$\cdot$m, occur for current combinations that
the controller does not produce. These static tests bound the instantaneous error during phase overlap at about
5--7\%.

To quantify the effect on drive-level results, the three phase currents from
the (uncoupled) drive simulation were applied \emph{simultaneously} in the FE
model at 24 rotor positions over one stroke. Mutual coupling and shared-yoke
saturation are then included, and the torque was compared with the uncoupled
FE-table torque at the same positions (Table~\ref{tab:coupled},
Fig.~\ref{fig:coupled}). Coupling changes the mean torque by $-1.5$\% to
$+0.7$\% and the ripple by at most 1.8 percentage points. The low ripple of the
TSF is preserved (7.3\% coupled against 6.4\% uncoupled at these sampling
points). The check is current-imposed, which is realistic under hysteresis
current control at low speed. In single-pulse operation, coupling would also
alter the current slightly, an effect not captured here. The uncoupled drive
model is therefore adequate for the predictions in this paper.

\subsection{Converter and Circuit Model}
Each phase obeys $d\psi/dt = v-R_{ph}i$, with the converter imposing
\begin{equation}
v=\begin{cases}
\;\;V_{dc}-2R_{ds}i & \text{both switches on (magnetize)}\\
-(R_{ds}i+V_D) & \text{one switch on (freewheel)}\\
-(V_{dc}+2V_D) & \text{both off, }i>0\text{ (demagnetize)}
\end{cases}
\label{eq:states}
\end{equation}
with $R_{ds}=4$~m$\Omega$ and $V_D=0.8$~V. The flux equation is integrated at
40~kHz or at least 1800 steps per period, or at 50 steps per PWM period for
fixed-frequency control. The current is obtained by inverting the tabulated
$\psi(i,\theta)$. One phase is simulated over successive rotor pole pitches
until the end-of-period flux repeats to within $10^{-4}\psi_{max}$. This
periodic steady state includes continuous conduction. The total torque is
$T_e(\theta)=\sum_k T_{ph}(\theta-k\varepsilon)$. The DC input power equals
the sum of electromagnetic power, copper loss and conduction loss to within
1.4\% from 80 to 1900~r/min. The residual is the error of the explicit time
integration and of the bilinear table interpolation.

\subsection{Loss Models and Efficiency Definitions}
Copper loss is $mR_{ph}I_{rms}^2$ at 100~$^\circ$C. Converter conduction loss is
integrated from \eqref{eq:states}. Switching energy is accumulated per transition
as $\tfrac12V_{dc}i\,t_{sw}$ ($t_{sw}=60$~ns) plus diode reverse recovery
$Q_{rr}V_{dc}$ ($Q_{rr}=40$~nC). Iron loss uses the Bertotti form~\cite{bertotti1988}
\begin{equation}
p_{fe}=k_{ns}\left(k_h f B^2 + k_e (fB)^2\right)\quad\text{(W/kg)},
\label{eq:iron}
\end{equation}
calibrated to 2.7~W/kg at 1.5~T/50~Hz (M270-35A), with $k_{ns}=1.3$ for the
unipolar, non-sinusoidal SRM flux. The efficiency boundaries are:
\begin{itemize}
\item motor efficiency $\eta_{mot}=P_{sh}/(P_{sh}+P_{cu}+P_{fe}+P_{mech})$;
\item system efficiency $\eta_{sys}=P_{sh}/P_{bat}$, from battery terminals to the motor shaft;
\item converter efficiency $\eta_{conv}=(P_{bat}-P_{conv})/P_{bat}$.
\end{itemize}
The gear is excluded from all three, so battery-to-wheel efficiency is
$\eta_{sys}\eta_g$.

\section{Control and Design Flow}
\label{sec:flow}

\subsection{Commutation-Angle Optimization}
The turn-on angle must lead the start of pole overlap $\theta_{ov}$ by roughly
the angle needed to build up the reference current in $L_u$:
\begin{equation}
\theta_{adv}\approx \frac{\omega L_u i_{ref}}{V_{dc}-(R_{ph}+2R_{ds})i_{ref}}.
\label{eq:adv}
\end{equation}
At each speed--torque point $(\omega,T^*)$ we solve
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
&\min_{\theta_{on},\theta_{off},i_{ref}} P_{bat}\\
&\text{s.t.}\;\; \bar T_e=T^*,\;\; i_{ref}\le I_{max},\;\; \theta_{off}-\theta_{on}\le 0.55\,\tau_r,
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:opt}
\end{equation}
where $\tau_r=2\pi/N_r$. The search is a grid followed by root-finding:
\begin{itemize}
\item $\theta_{off}$ takes four values at 55--95\% of the interval from overlap
to alignment (six values at 45--95\% for maximum torque).
\item $\theta_{on}$ takes three values in $[\theta_{ov}-1.6\theta_{adv},\theta_{ov}]$
plus four dwell-defined values ($\theta_{off}-d\,\tau_r$, $d=0.35$--$0.5$).
\item For each angle pair, $i_{ref}$ is found by Brent's method to 0.02~A /
0.1\% tolerance.
\end{itemize}
To check convergence, a 4$\times$ denser grid (9 advance values, 15
$\theta_{off}$ values and 13 dwell values) improves $\eta_{sys}$ by at most 1.5
points (rated point) and the maximum torque by at most 2.9\% (base speed). The
efficiency map is therefore slightly conservative.

The dwell limit was chosen from a sweep. At base speed, the maximum torque
rises from 2.07~N$\cdot$m (dwell $\le0.40\tau_r$) to 3.95~N$\cdot$m
($0.55\tau_r$) and falls to 3.77~N$\cdot$m for longer dwells. With longer
dwells the tail current enters the negative-torque region. At $0.55\tau_r$ and
above base speed, the optimum runs in mild continuous conduction, with a
minimum current of 1.3--1.7~A (about 5\% of peak), which the periodic solver
handles. In a controller the solution becomes a 2-D look-up table
$(\theta_{on},\theta_{off},i_{ref})=f(T^*,\omega)$~\cite{kjaer1997,xue2010}.

\section{Predicted Performance (FE-Based)}

\subsection{Current and Torque Waveforms}
Fig.~\ref{fig:waveforms} shows the steady-state waveforms. At launch
(80~r/min), soft chopping holds the current at 29.2~A, and the mean torque is
6.63~N$\cdot$m. This exceeds the 5.93-N$\cdot$m requirement by 11.6\%. At the
rated point (1608~r/min) the turn-on angle is advanced to $-2.9^\circ$ and the
drive operates essentially in single-pulse mode. Throughout this paper,
torque ripple is the peak-to-peak value normalized to the mean,
\begin{equation}
\Delta T=\frac{\max_\theta T_e(\theta)-\min_\theta T_e(\theta)}{\bar T_e}\times100\%,
\label{eq:ripple}
\end{equation}
evaluated over one electrical period of the total torque $T_e$. With this
definition a waveform that touches zero once per stroke has
$\Delta T\geq100$\%, which explains the large values for current-reference
control. The ripple with current-reference control is 60\% at launch and 87\%
at the rated point; Section~\ref{sec:tsf} addresses it.

The launch efficiency in Table~\ref{tab:ops} (17.7\% motor, 15.9\% system) is
low because the output power at 80~r/min is only 54~W while the copper loss is
set by the 29-A launch current. This is a short-duration, torque-dominated
condition (a few seconds per start) and is not representative of
steady-state riding; its thermal consequence is evaluated in
Section~\ref{sec:thermal}, and the continuous operating points are the hill,
rated and cruise points.

\subsection{Converter and DC-Link Design}
The asymmetric half bridge (two switches and two diodes per phase) is the only
common SRM converter that provides $+V_{dc}$, $0$ and $-V_{dc}$ independently in
every phase~\cite{krishnan2001,miller1993}. These are the states that soft
chopping, fast demagnetization and TSF require. With the FE characteristic,
the ratings are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
\item device voltage $\ge1.5V_{bat,max}=63$~V, so 100-V MOSFETs are selected;
\item $I_{max}=29.2$~A, with a device RMS current of 20.6~A at launch;
\item average battery current of 8.1~A at the rated point and 9.4~A at launch.
\end{itemize}

The DC-link capacitor is designed in two steps. The first-cut charge bound
assumes that the battery supplies only the mean current:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
C&\ge\frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta V},\qquad q(t)=\int_0^t\big(i_{dc}-\bar i_{dc}\big)\,d\tau,\\
\Delta Q&=\max_t q(t)-\min_t q(t).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:dq}
\end{equation}
At the rated point (stroke frequency 643~Hz), \eqref{eq:dq} gives 1.56~mF for
5\% ripple. At launch the stroke frequency is only 32~Hz and the bound becomes a
meaningless 30~mF, because in reality the battery supplies most of that
low-frequency ripple. The second step therefore resolves $i_{dc}(t)$ into
harmonics and divides each one between the capacitor branch
$Z_c=R_{ESR}+1/(j\omega C)$ and the battery branch $Z_b=R_b+j\omega L_b$. We
assume $R_{ESR}=20$~m$\Omega$, $R_b=0.15~\Omega$ (10S pack) and $L_b=1~\mu$H
(cable). Table~\ref{tab:dclink} gives the results:
\begin{itemize}
\item At the rated point, 1~mF keeps the link ripple at 1.7~V peak to peak

outside the scope of this paper.

\subsection{Predicted Thermal Envelope}

lumped-parameter model and define a predicted thermal envelope, not validated
temperature rises. A four-node network replaces the single-node estimate of the first version.
The nodes are the winding, stator, rotor and hub shell. The conductances are
estimated from the geometry:
\begin{itemize}
\item winding to stator: 25~W/K (slot liner, 0.35~mm, 0.25~W/mK);
\item stator to shell: 23~W/K (contact, 1500~W/m$^2$K);
\item rotor to shell: 0.6~W/K;
\item shell to ambient: $hA_{sh}$, with $A_{sh}=0.075$~m$^2$.

\section{Torque-Ripple Reduction With Torque-Sharing Functions}
\label{sec:tsf}
A TSF assigns each phase a torque reference $T_k^*=T^*f_k(\theta)$. The rising
function of the incoming phase and the falling function of the outgoing phase
overlap over $\theta_{ov}$ and always sum to one~\cite{xue2009,vujicic2012}. We
implemented linear, cubic and exponential shapes. Each phase reference is
converted to a current reference through the inverse FE static characteristic
$i(T,\theta)$ and tracked by a three-level hysteresis controller (+$V_{dc}$, 0,
$-V_{dc}$). For each shape, the turn-on angle (3--7.5$^\circ$) and overlap
(1.5--4.5$^\circ$) were chosen for minimum ripple, and the torque command was
trimmed so that the mean torque equals the target.

Table~\ref{tab:tsf} and Fig.~\ref{fig:tsf} show the results. Below 500~r/min,
the cubic TSF reduces the peak-to-peak ripple from about 197\% to 10--13\% at
200~r/min and to 33\% at 500~r/min. At 800~r/min and 1.5~N$\cdot$m the ripple
falls from 83\% to 39\%. The price is a 12--28\% higher RMS current and 7--12
points of system efficiency. Above about 800~r/min, or 3~N$\cdot$m at
800~r/min, no TSF solution exists within $I_{max}$ at 36~V, because the back-EMF
prevents the falling phase from following its reference. This is the
well-known speed limit of TSF control~\cite{ye2015,xue2009}, and it is
particularly restrictive at low DC-link voltage. A practical controller is
therefore hybrid: TSF for launch, walk-assist and low-speed climbing, where
ripple is felt most and power is small, and current-reference angle control
above about 12~km/h. Coupled three-phase FE solves (Section~\ref{sec:mutual}) confirm that the
TSF ripple reduction survives mutual coupling (6.4\% $\rightarrow$ 7.3\% at the
200-r/min point).

\section{Route Energy Evaluation}
\label{sec:route}
Three standardized missions are simulated quasi-statically:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{flat urban}: 6.4~km on level roads with six stops;
\item \emph{hilly urban}: 6.4~km with five stops, a 700-m climb at 6\% and a
900-m descent at $-4$\% (Fig.~\ref{fig:cycle});
\item \emph{aggressive}: 5.4~km with climbs of 6--10\% totalling 2.2~km and
descents at $-4$\% and $-7$\%.
\end{itemize}
In all three, the rider supplies up to 100~W, force-limited at low speed. The
motor supplies the remaining tractive power below 25~km/h, with efficiency
interpolated from the FE-based maps, and braking is mechanical.

On the hilly mission (18.9~min), the predictions at a fixed 36~V are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
\item the rider contributes 25.5~Wh and the motor 23.4~Wh at the shaft;
\item the motor draws 32.7~Wh from the battery, a cycle-average $\eta_{sys}$ of 71.4\%;
\item consumption is 5.12~Wh/km.
\end{itemize}
Table~\ref{tab:missions} compares the three missions, with and without the

\emph{estimated} range to the 30-V cut-off is 142, 68 and 32~km. These figures

\section{Design Trade-Offs and Test of the Corner-Speed Rule}
\label{sec:trade}
Table~\ref{tab:trade} and Fig.~\ref{fig:trade} vary the gear ratio $G$ and the
peak electric loading $A$. Each machine is redesigned and the current is
re-sized. The analytical characteristic is used here for computational reasons;
it reproduces the FE rated efficiency of the baseline to within 1 point
(Table~\ref{tab:anfe}), so the trends are reliable even though absolute values
carry the uncertainty of Table~\ref{tab:feaval}. Three observations stand out.
\begin{enumerate}
\item The required peak current (for the launch torque, before the 10\%
converter margin) lies between 25.2 and 30.6~A across a 3.9-fold range of active
mass. Fig.~\ref{fig:corner}(a) shows this. A linear fit of current against mass
has a slope of $-0.06$~A/kg ($-0.2$\%/kg) and $R^2=0.004$, so the current is
statistically independent of machine size. The loop-utilization factor is
$k_c=0.60\pm0.03$ (mean $\pm$ standard deviation, range 0.54--0.66), and the rule
$I_{pk}=P_c/(\bar k_cV_{dc,min})=27.8$~A reproduces every design to within
10.3\%. To test the voltage dependence, the baseline frame ($G=8$,
$A=45$~kA/m) was redesigned for 24-, 36- and 48-V packs ($V_{dc,min}=20$, 30
and 40~V; 27, 41 and 54 turns per pole). The required current falls from 42.3
to 27.9 to 21.2~A, a log--log slope of $-1.002$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:corner}(b)), with
$k_c=0.59$--$0.60$ in all three cases. Both tests support \eqref{eq:invariant}
within the scope of this design family (one-stroke dwell, same pole
configuration and loading coefficients).
\item Rated efficiency is governed mainly by electric loading, i.e., by active
mass. For $G=8$, lowering $A$ from 60 to 35~kA/m raises $\eta_{sys}$ from 75.1\%
to 80.3\% while active mass grows from 2.5 to 4.4~kg.
\item At similar mass, the gear ratio matters little. The $G=8$/45~kA/m
baseline (3.40~kg, 78.8\%) and the $G=11$/35~kA/m variant (3.17~kg, 78.2\%)
are within one point. The ratio should therefore be chosen on gear cost, noise
and diameter. The single-stage 8:1 baseline favors cost, and the 11:1 variant
gives the smallest diameter for a given efficiency.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Position Sensing}
Commutation needs rotor position to about 1$^\circ$ mechanical. Three Hall
sensors on a target ring give 15$^\circ$ edges. Between edges, the angle can be
extrapolated using speed and acceleration estimated from the edge timing. This
is adequate when the speed change between edges is small, which holds above
about 50~r/min given the vehicle inertia. The quantization error and the
sensor-alignment tolerance (typically $\pm$1$^\circ$) appear directly as
commutation-angle errors, and their effect follows from the sensitivity of
the optimized operating points to $\theta_{off}$. At standstill and during a
hill start the Hall states only identify a 15$^\circ$ sector, so the controller
must excite the phase pair that is known to give positive torque throughout
that sector and switch to interpolation once two edges have been seen.
Direction reversal (walking backward) is detected from the Hall state
sequence. Sensorless schemes~\cite{ehsani2002} are attractive for sealed hubs
but must achieve reliable stand-still starts on a grade, which is exactly the
launch condition that sizes the drive.

\subsection{Air Gap and Mechanical Tolerances}
The FE results in Table~\ref{tab:gap} quantify the air-gap sensitivity. At
0.35~mm the mean torque at 30~A drops by 4.0\% (8.3\% at 15~A) and $L_a$ by
10\%. At 0.40~mm the torque drops by 8.2\% (16\% at 15~A). Table~\ref{tab:stack}
gives an \emph{assumed} tolerance allocation that is typical for small motors
and must be confirmed for a specific design. Worst-case stacking reduces the
local gap to 0.215~mm, and the root-sum-square (RSS) combination to 0.26~mm.
This corresponds to a 13--28\% eccentricity with corresponding unbalanced
magnetic pull, so the rotor bearings and the axle must be dimensioned for it.
Because the wheel loads in a geared hub are carried by the hub bearings
rather than by the motor bearings, the remaining contributors are
manufacturing tolerances.

\subsection{Equal-Envelope Benchmark Against a Surface-PM Machine}
\label{sec:pm}
Catalog efficiencies of PM e-bike motors are not comparable with the present
predictions, because their measurement boundaries, voltages, temperatures and
test methods differ or are not stated. We therefore sized a first-order
analytic surface-PM (SPM) benchmark to the \emph{same} constraints:
\begin{itemize}
\item the same mission, envelope ($D_o=135.3$~mm, $L=35.9$~mm), gear and
battery;
\item the same MOSFETs, iron-loss coefficients and mechanical loss;
\item the same efficiency boundary and route model.
\end{itemize}
The benchmark is a 12-slot/10-pole concentrated-winding machine (winding factor
0.933) with N42SH magnets ($B_r=1.17$~T at 100~$^\circ$C, 3~mm thick, 0.83 pole
arc) and a 0.5-mm air gap. Its turns follow the same voltage logic as the SRM:
the no-load line-to-line EMF at 25~km/h equals the 30-V minimum battery voltage.
It is driven by sinusoidal $I_d=0$ control from a six-switch inverter at
16~kHz. Its iron loss uses the same Bertotti coefficients ($k_{ns}=1$), and an
assumed magnet eddy-current loss of 10\% of the stator iron loss is added. The
model neglects saturation, slotting harmonics and field weakening. It
therefore matches the fidelity of the SRM's \emph{analytical} model, and
Table~\ref{tab:pm} lists both SRM models.

The benchmark gives an unambiguous result, which we report as it stands. At the
same envelope, the SPM is predicted to be about 11--12 points more efficient at
the rated point and far better at launch, where the SRM's copper loss is ten
times higher. It also uses 4\% and 20\% less energy on the hilly and
aggressive missions. On the flat, light-assist mission the SRM uses 18\% less
energy. The motor then delivers small torques for long periods, and the
SPM's magnet-driven iron loss (about 18~W at 25~km/h, independent of load)
dominates, while the SRM's iron loss scales with excitation. The SPM also
dissipates about 20~W when coasting without a freewheel clutch. Geared hubs
normally include such a clutch, but direct-drive hubs do not. Against these
efficiency deficits, the SRM offers zero magnet content (0.16~kg of NdFeB
avoided), robustness to temperature and overspeed, phase-independent fault
tolerance and a simpler rotor. Its case for pedelecs therefore rests on cost,
supply-chain and robustness grounds and on light-assist use, not on peak
efficiency. Because the SPM model is first-order, its efficiencies are likely
optimistic by a few points, but not by enough to reverse the comparison at the
rated point.

\subsection{Limitations and Validation Status}
\label{sec:limits}
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{No experimental results.} All performance, efficiency, range and
temperature values are model predictions. Prototype dynamometer tests (phase
voltage and current, torque and ripple, DC input power, efficiency map,
temperature rise and acoustic noise) are the next step of this work.
\item \emph{Magnetics.} 2-D FE neglects end effects except for the analytical
end-winding term. Axial fringing in a 36-mm stack typically adds a few percent
of inductance. Mutual coupling is not included in the drive model; coupled FE solves bound
its effect on mean torque at 1.5\% (Section~\ref{sec:mutual}).
\item \emph{Losses.} Iron loss uses a peak-flux-density model with a
non-sinusoidal factor ($\pm$30\%).
\item \emph{Thermal network.} The thermal parameters are geometry-based
estimates that have not been calibrated.
\item \emph{Route model.} The route model is quasi-static; battery sag is
included through an equivalent circuit (Section~\ref{sec:battery}) but
electrical and mechanical transients are not.
\item \emph{Acoustics.} Acoustic noise and radial-force vibration are outside
the present modeling scope, and no claim is made that the design is
acoustically acceptable.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
This paper has presented an analytical, FE and simulation-based design
methodology for a switched reluctance motor drive for a 250-W, 36-V pedelec.
All results are model predictions; no prototype has been measured.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Corner-speed rule.} The phase turns are chosen at the
mission-derived corner speed $n_c=P_{pk}/T_{pk}$. For the specified 36-V, 500-W
corner power, this approximately halves the peak current relative to sizing
the turns for launch torque at 25~km/h. The relation $P_c=k_cV_{dc}I_{pk}$
recasts the classical converter volt-ampere argument for sizing. Within this
design family it holds with $k_c=0.60\pm0.03$, shows no dependence of current
on machine mass ($R^2=0.004$ over a 3.9-fold mass range), and gives
$I_{pk}\propto V^{-1.00}$ across 24--48-V designs.
\item \emph{FE-based numerical verification.} Nonlinear 2-D FE showed that the
analytical model errs by up to 23\% in flux linkage and 17\% in mean torque, so
FE tables were used for all drive-level predictions. Coupled three-phase FE
solves changed mean torque by at most 1.5\%.
\item \emph{Predicted performance.} The 12/8, $\varnothing$135-mm, 3.4-kg
(active) machine with an 8:1 gear and a 100-V MOSFET asymmetric bridge meets
the launch torque at 26.5~A with an 11.6\% margin at any battery voltage from
30 to 42~V. It reaches 80.2\% motor and 77.9\% system efficiency at the rated
point ($\pm$2 points for $\pm$30\% iron-loss uncertainty), and consumes
2.5--10.9~Wh/km on flat to aggressive missions.
\item \emph{Torque ripple.} TSF control reduces ripple to 10--33\% below
500~r/min but is infeasible above about 800~r/min at 36~V, which motivates a
hybrid controller.
\item \emph{Converter and thermal.} Switching losses are negligible at 36~V up to
20~kHz, and the DC link is ripple-current-limited. In the predicted thermal
envelope, only sustained climbing in hot climates approaches the insulation
limit.
\item \emph{PM benchmark.} An equal-envelope analytic SPM is predicted to be
about 12 points more efficient at the rated point and to use less energy on
hilly and aggressive missions. The SRM uses 18\% less energy on flat
light-assist riding and needs no magnets.
\end{itemize}
The SRM is therefore a credible magnet-free option for pedelecs whose case
rests on cost, supply chain, robustness and light-assist use rather than on
peak efficiency. Prototype validation of these predictions is the subject of
ongoing work.
